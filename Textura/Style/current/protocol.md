# Style operating protocol

## Sample selection and record status

1. Begin with the author's selected samples and the current task. Record immutable Writing or Sources/Reading versions, locators, language, genre, completeness and processing restrictions. Keep material not selected for profile analysis outside that analysis; a reviewer encountering a draft does not automatically make it a style sample.
2. Record why each sample was selected: representative work, approved draft, trial, imitation exercise, collaborative text or another stated purpose. Preserve known author/AI contribution information and unknowns. A selected experiment may be analysed without becoming evidence of a lasting preference.
3. Keep observations, author-confirmed preferences, provisional goals and proposed experiments distinct. Separately mark records superseded when appropriate. Include examples, counterexamples and author decision references; one adopted edit is not a permanent style rule.
4. Treat the author's present intuition as a provisional goal when that is what was expressed. Do not relabel an aspiration as an observed trait or make a personal choice the shared core's default.
5. Append revised findings with predecessor references. New draft or sample versions may change earlier findings; retain the earlier analysis with its actual input versions. Inherit `local_only` restrictions into excerpts, summaries, comparisons and statistics.

## Scope and voice layers

1. Establish the author, work, narrator and character layers needed for this task. They are optional scopes, not compulsory forms to fill before writing. Refer to Fiction's narrator and character identities when applicable.
2. Record applicable language, genre, passage, scene, timeline or alternative. A character's deliberate evasiveness or a work's elaborate narration need not follow the author's general tendency toward brevity.
3. Read the scope and exceptions before applying a preference. Do not flatten all voices or infer automatic precedence when scopes conflict; explain the tension and seek a decision only where it affects the proposed work.
4. Examine wording, syntax, rhythm, imagery and tone as well as point of view, information order, scene presentation and development of ideas. Style owns analysis of the method and effect; Fiction owns the actual story structure, events, knowledge states and change dependencies.

## General writing and revision

1. Identify the work version, language, genre mixture, intended audience and author-confirmed constraints. Prose can combine narrative, observation, lyric reflection and argument; do not force a fixed category.
2. Examine possible function before correcting an unusual expression. Separate grammar/usage concerns, clarity suggestions, stylistic alternatives and possible deliberate departures. Explain likely effects and reading friction; keep uncertain intention explicit.
3. Where useful, offer an alternative retaining the distinctive feature and a more conventional option. Explain gains, losses and retained meaning. Writing owns candidate text versions; Style links its rationale to them.
4. When a change affects a factual claim, quotation, qualifier or inference, flag it for Evidence/review. When it affects story events, knowledge or structure, flag it for Fiction. Do not classify these changes as cosmetic.
5. In learning languages, prioritize natural expression and work quality. Explain choices and offer simpler alternatives when useful, without automatically limiting the work to the author's current proficiency.
6. Suggest adjustments with a purpose, cost and example. The author may retain deliberate inconsistency or ambiguity. Record meaningful decisions in State.

## Reference methods and optional statistics

1. Identify the actual edition, source version and passages read, including whether the text is an original, translation or adaptation. A partial sample supports a bounded description, not a whole-career portrait.
2. Explain methods through concrete examples, counterexamples and possible effects. Distinguish textual observations from hypotheses about an effect. Do not infer an author's psychology, biography or intentions without appropriate evidence.
3. For translations, name the translator and translation version when known. Do not assign the translation's lexical, syntactic or rhythmic choices directly to the original author. If the original was not inspected, say so; structural observations must also respect abridgement, adaptation and extraction limitations.
4. Keep description, comparison and proposed adaptation separate. Offer ways to try a method in the author's own work without treating the reference author's practice as the target norm.
5. Use statistics only when helpful to an actual question. Record corpus selection, version hashes, extraction limits, metric definitions, segmentation/counting rules, language handling and the method or tool actually used. Provide reproducible procedure references when calculations were performed.
6. Compare suitable samples and return to the passages. Translation, language, genre, length and dialogue conventions can confound comparisons. A metric is not a quality score, personality measure or proof of authorial intention. Mark unperformed calculations as planned, never as results.

## Optional practice and work analysis

1. Let the author choose current-work analysis, independent practice or both. There is no universal starting mode or priority.
2. For practice, agree on a modest target and useful comparison, such as narrative distance, sentence rhythm or differentiated dialogue. Style owns the exercise brief and effect discussion; Writing owns each actual text version.
3. Keep exercise results separate from confirmed preferences. Offer a next experiment when useful; do not automatically schedule a programme, claim progress or require exercises before work can continue.
4. Only author-selected exercise versions enter a personal sample catalogue. Selecting a sample and confirming a preference are separate decisions.

## Free verse

1. Freeze the poem version and check that its reading representation preserves the meaningful line breaks and stanza gaps. If extraction cannot preserve them, identify the limitation and use an author-provided faithful text or supported document view before judging form. For the first reading, do not load author-intent notes. If intention was already visible, disclose that the reading is not fully blind.
2. Offer a situated possible reading: imagery, emotional movement, sound/rhythm, line breaks, repetition, silence and productive or obstructive ambiguity. Identify textual grounds and alternative readings.
3. Preserve that first reading before discussing intent. Compare it with the intent the author chooses to share; differences can be useful, not automatically defects.
4. Apply the three review perspectives with literary criteria: strengths in the poem's effects, tensions or weak choices, then whether criticisms misread purposeful features or impose unsuitable standards.
5. Give candidate changes and explain their losses as well as gains. Do not enforce regular meter or rhyme on free verse without author intent. Factual quotations or historical references still use Evidence.

Poetry interpretation is not evidence of the author's psychology or biography. Keep textual response distinct from unsupported personal inference.
